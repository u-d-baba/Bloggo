**SPOILER ALERT**: The article ahead has spoilers for the 2019 Gujarati film *Hellaro*. It's one of my Letterboxd top four, so I recommend that you watch it before reading on.

---

Fifty years ago, in 1976, Swedish pop group ABBA released their new album *Arrival*, which includes the track *Dancing Queen*, widely regarded as one of the *happiest* songs of all time. Here is an excerpt from the lyrics:


> You can dance, you can jive \
> Having the time of your life \
> Ooooh, See that girl, watch that scene \
> Digging the dancing queen

Set a year earlier, in 1975, [*Hellaro*](https://letterboxd.com/film/hellaro/) tells the story of oppressed women in a village in the Rann of Kutch. It opens with the women confined to their huts while the men perform garba outside, praying to the goddess for rain. In the background plays the song **સપના વિનાની રાત** *(A night without dreams)*, which contains:

> તારી વીજળી ભૂંસી નાખજે \
> *Kill your spark*
>
> તારા પગ ના ઝાંઝર રોકજે \
> *Don't let your anklets clink*
>
> ને માવડી પાછે માંગજે ખાલી રાત રે \
> *Ask the goddess for an empty night*
>
> સપના વિના ની આખી રાત \
> *A night without dreams*

The idea for this post came to me when I rewatched the movie a couple of days ago, a few weeks after the national capital echoed with women-led protests demanding accountability for women's safety. The rewatch made me realise that a fictional village in 1970s India was not very different from an actual urban ecosystem in India in 2026 in how it treated women. How is it that so little has changed over the years? What follows is my commentary on the movie and on the women's safety crisis that has plagued our country for decades.

---

> A twenty-year-old Assamese woman was found dead in Andhra Pradesh a few days ago. \
> A private university in Punjab was ravaged after a female student was raped within its campus. \
> A seventeen-year-old girl was allegedly gang-raped in Delhi by three men pretending to be police officers.

These are some headlines I can pull off the top of my head, and the tragedy is that all of these events happened within a month or so. And will such a list ever come to a full stop? Realistically, I don't think so. Not yet.

In my country, I have seen such events follow a sort of life cycle to their relevance. The news comes out. Some people try to gain social awareness points by posting an AI-slop poster about women's safety on their Instagram stories. Media channels are all over the case; if it's something major, there's a protest to cover as well. Someone is apprehended. He's put in jail. The masses sympathise with the victims and forget about it as soon as the protest candles burn out. New day, new case.

I may sound sarcastic when I say this, but the way it stands right now, only goddesses can save their daughters and break us all out of this vicious loop.

---

> "જમાનો નાગો થઇ ને નાચે તોહ આપડે પણ નાગા થઈને નાચવાનું?" \
> *If the times dance naked, must we dance naked too?*

The village in the movie is run by men who are very uptight about protecting their *culture*. A *culture* which involves worshipping the goddess with utmost devotion, while their wives and daughters aren't allowed to leave their huts for anything but fetching water from miles away across the arid desert. A *culture* which just sees women as instruments for producing offspring. It is a *culture* built on the foundations of pathetic superstitions that promote misogyny and patriarchy.

Put simply, patriarchy refers to the idea that a woman is somehow *less* than a man. And this is not even an India problem: subtle signs of it exist everywhere in the world if you look closely. Women's Grand Slam tennis matches, for instance, are still best-of-three while men's are best-of-five. However, I feel that the problem is much more severe in our country.

Going by my personal experience, though it may be shaped by the religious constructs I grew up around, I believe that arranged marriages in India are one of the key reasons this epidemic is still unresolved.

Now, I am not against arranged marriage; Hinge and Bumble are anyway not working for me. What I hate is the manner in which they are executed. I have noticed that in most cases, the foundations of such relationships are *transactional*: a contract of sorts whose terms encourage patriarchal narratives:

* the bride will change her last name
* the bride will leave her house
* her family will pay the dowry, and so on...

What's more ironic is that society likes to call it a match between equals (equals in faith, caste, class and other useless attributes), but the very beginning of the dynamic remains heavily lopsided. I have also had this discussion with a couple of my friends: are arranged marriages maybe just cases of [Stockholm syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_syndrome) in disguise? Also, since they are the norm in our country, the *"men better"* mindset spreads like a genetic disease, whose extreme stages show up in cruel symptoms like domestic violence and marital rape.

Another thing that hurts is that this idea is hammered into women's heads in such a manner that they become its preachers as well. Maybe this is the origin story of some of those older women who too try to blame a rape victim by asking "What was she doing out alone at night?" and "What was she wearing?", because they have been force-fed the belief that a woman has practically no life of her own.

This oppression of thought is portrayed in the movie in a rather brutal manner: the women have been rewired to the extent that they blame themselves for anything that goes awry in their village. They fast because it has not rained for years. When someone's newborn dies, they blame it on the fact that they had been dancing secretly while out fetching water. Of course these are rather extreme superstitions, but fragments of them still exist in our society.

You might think that times have changed. Young women are more independent than ever; they are pursuing higher education, working jobs and creating their own identities. One account from my life led me to rethink that narrative completely. It was when I was with two of my female friends (IITK grads), discussing life while sitting in Central Park in Delhi. Amid all the talk, it stuck with me when they both said, "25-26 ki age me family se shaadi ka pressure aane hi lagta hai" (*Family will start pressuring for marriage once a girl turns 25-26*).

> *Hey Bhagla, tell us about that movie "Baabi"…*
>
> અલા એ, બોબી છે! \

For decades, Indian cinema has been producing movies that bring male-centric stories to life, in which the female lead is merely a decorative showpiece in the publicity campaign. I don't even blame the creatives involved: the market dictates it. What good will your film do at the box office if there's no *Basanti* dancing to tease the men?

The objectification of women in movies began way back and is, sadly, still thriving. One reason, in Satyajit Ray's words as he defended his movi Devi during an interview in 1989, is that the Indian movie-going audience is *"fairly backward and unsophisticated"*. They don't see movies as a medium that demands critical thought. They don't realise that movies have the power to alter their minds forever. They don't understand that movies are just a creative construction and not reality.

Now imagine such an audience being fed movies like [Animal](https://letterboxd.com/film/animal-2023/), which go above and beyond in endorsing toxic masculinity: it's like washing their brains with mud. And it's not just restricted to movies. Consider this: [*"Baby Marvake Maanegi"*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AtUh3lJeac8) is an actual title of a track by one of India's best-known rap artists, Raftaar, and has millions of views on YouTube.

Though I might sound like one, I don't intend to act as the moral police. I truly believe that art can take multiple forms and I am not blaming the creatives behind such explicit productions, but at the same time, we cannot really afford to let such forms of art loose on an average Indian who is quick to take things literally and always acts as if they are living on a Bollywood set.

The obvious solution is to have stringent age restrictions on all forms of content produced, and the state must prioritise it. We must also realise that, apart from the content being produced, the ease with which it can be consumed is also concerning, thanks to the dirt-cheap internet in our country. I am an enjoyer of the high-speed internet I get for pennies, but I think in a broader sense, it might be doing more harm than good.

> મારા રોયા પંદર મહિના થઇ ગયા, હવા માંજ ગોળીબાર થતો લાગે છે, પરિણામ તોહ આવતું નથી \
> *It's been fifteen months, and it seems you're firing into the air, with no results [pregnancy]*
>
> બંદૂક તોહ સાચી છે ને? કે ટીકડી વાડી? \
> *Is your gun [d\*ck] even real, or a cap gun?*

The above is a dialogue among men from the movie. It's all innuendo: they speak about intercourse similar to the way characters in *Harry Potter* speak about Voldemort, except here it is the deed that must not be named. How ironic it is that the birthplace of the Kama Sutra has evolved to host a society which sees sex as taboo; birds and bees are just flying animals for Indian children.

We've entered a new technological era, and the sight of a child glued to a screen is becoming more common than ever. It's highly likely that children don't realise that the reality of the world they're living in, and its diabolical state, lies a few clicks away. Unsupervised access will expose them to subjects they shouldn't be exposed to at such a delicate age; and I say this with conviction because of the psychological engineering that goes into the algorithms running our online feeds and suggestions.

Can we really let someone's careless parenting today translate to someone else's lifelong trauma tomorrow? These children might grow up to join the millions of sexually illiterate young people in this country, who have been greatly misguided by a fake teacher in the form of online pornography sites. Their whole idea of sex has been shaped by hundreds of unrealistic productions, where fundamentals like consent, protection and communication are almost entirely absent. For girls, that might mean never understanding how sex is supposed to be in reality, and being sexually exploited without realising it. For some boys, that might provide fertile ground to grow as catcallers, molesters, or even rapists.

But what do we do when the parents themselves are not educated enough? Who will get it done: schools? That is ironic because our textbooks are being rewritten by the current government[^1], and they I doubt they have room for necessary sex-ed for children.

---

The patriarchal society we live in, and the kind of media it mass-produces, combined with a lack of critical thought, sexual illiteracy and careless parenting, can easily breed a rapist mindset. And however depressing I may sound, the truth is that it is close to impossible to inculcate critical thinking in, and educate, over a billion people who have already been brainwashed by their *traditions* and *culture*. But on an optimistic note, I like to believe that baby steps can be taken to slightly (very, very slightly) alleviate this problem: ban online pornography (actually ban it, not just in name) and include proper sex-ed in the school curriculum.

I think it can be fairly concluded that the ecosystem of our country does promote, to quite some extent, the growth of demonic mindsets. But that doesn't excuse the crimes. The reason these monsters are still going rabid in our society is that no one is taking charge to muzzle them.

Blame the bloody government here.

Now, this is not coming from my anti-BJP stance; we all know who was in power when Nirbhaya happened. It comes from the fact that it is extremely difficult for us as individuals to cause a paradigm shift without the help of the machinery of the state. We must confront any authority in power that is shying away from this mess we are living in.

So, what should the government do? We already have police and women's helpline speed dials, but women in India still feel unsafe, so a decent start would be to make those effective. Next, in my opinion, would be to chop off the genitalia of all convicted rapists; and I consider this a much more severe penalty than hanging because it would strip the person of all that ego and manliness which would have led him to commit such a horror. Let him also live with trauma for the rest of his life.

Altering the laws will not do much on its own until the fear actually sets in among such losers, and that will only happen when justice is delivered on time. If a widely discussed case like Nirbhaya took more than seven years for its perpetrators to be hanged, how can anyone trust the legal system of this country in such matters? Still, if it happens, hardening those laws would be a step forward.

I won't elaborate on other matters surrounding this issue, like corruption and how many of our public *servants* are as good as criminal overlords; that rant could go on very long.

It is unfortunate that the recent Lady Shri Ram College protests in Delhi were clouded by the CJP drama. I pray that the need to organise a protest never arises, but if it does, I hope it results in some tangible change. Apart from that, I think it's easy to do our bit, and it can be something as simple as taking an unsupervised phone away from your little cousin, calling out your friends and family if they reek of patriarchy in any form, and, most importantly, speaking out if you see something wrong happening, because, as it is mentioned in the Bhagavad Gita, inaction is not neutrality.

---

I used to think nothing could give me goosebumps like *Interstellar*'s docking scene, until I watched the climax of *Hellaro*. In the final act, the women finally break their patriarchal shackles, braving the men to come together for a round of garba in front of their goddess. The drum reverberates in the background and it starts raining, as a fierce song, **હૈયા** *(Heart)*, plays...

> છોડ્યાં મેં ઉંબરાને છોડી મેં પાળ \
> *I left the gate* \
> છોડી તેં પાથરેલી આખી જંજાળ \
> *[and with it] I left the troubles you created* 

> હવે મારી ઝાંઝરીને બોલવાની છૂટ \
> ***Now my anklets are free to sing*** 

> ખીલેથી છૂટ્યા છે ઓરતાંના ઘણ \
> *My longings are free from the nails* \

I end this with a message to all of my female readers in India. I honestly can only guess at the disgust you may feel when a low-life leers at you, or when some regressive busybodies raise their brows at your wardrobe; the anxiety your parents go through until you're back home safely; or how it feels to be alone on the streets like prey, with a predator possibly lurking in any dark corner.

But I urge you to fight. I know you've been drafted into a war that shouldn't have existed in the first place, but you must fight. Fight, because the roots of this evil run deep, and if you don't fight now, the weed will only spread. Fight, not just for yourself, but for the generations of girls who are yet to be born. It might be hard to believe, but I am sure that there is some good out there, or else the world would have ended in shambles by now, and that goodness will always support you. Fight, because you're powerful: slap that ogler, pepper-spray (in fact, just fucking puncture the eyes of) those monsters, bust their fucking balls.

Fight, for your right to live; your right to *dance*. Because what good is this world without its dancing queens?
